**"I thought I was fixing a relationship, but I was actually rationally dismantling a person."**

In the glow of a screen late at night, I confessed this belated realization to Gemini. Even at this moment, I'm still processing my feelings through rational organization — trying to figure out in a way I can understand what exactly went wrong in our relationship. But it's not that I lack emotion. I still miss her, I still like her — I just know she won't accept me again, and I hope I can dissect myself to avoid repeating the same mistakes in the future.

As an INTJ whose mind habitually systematizes and modularizes the world, my brain operates with a single logic: identify the problem, define the variables, propose an optimization plan. This way of thinking is an infallible compass in the workplace, but in an intimate relationship, it is a cold surgical scalpel.

## I. The INTJ's Arrogance: The Unequal Power Disguised as "For Your Own Good"

In my logical world, loving someone means "wanting them to become better." When I saw my partner stagnating in some areas or falling short, my first reaction was not to soothe, but to activate "Debugger Mode."

I would unconsciously become a "coach" in the relationship, trying to guide her toward what I considered the ideal lifestyle, optimizing the details I deemed insufficient. Yet this role, by its very nature, presupposes a hierarchical power dynamic. I was too eager to achieve "better outcomes," ignoring that she was not a student to be taught or improved, but a soul who needed to be seen.

Those suggestions I thought were well-intentioned — what she heard was: "The way you are right now is not good enough." The confidence she once admired in my eyes had, at some point, become arrogance and conceit.

## II. System Conflict: When "Evolution Logic" Meets "Acceptance Logic"

Through this deep post-mortem with Gemini, I realized that the underlying logic of this relationship's collapse was the complete incompatibility of two "core operating systems":

**My system (INTJ): Like → Observe → Optimize → For a better future.** For me, love is "collaborative evolution." If I see your bug and don't point it out, that's irresponsible. I invested enormous "R&D resources" analyzing her upbringing, lifestyle, and wardrobe because I wanted our system performance to reach its optimum.

**Her system (ENFJ): Like → Feel → Settle → Enjoy who you are now.** For her, the essence of love is "complete acceptance and emotional stability." She sought a sense of security free from judgment. When I attempted to "optimize" her, her system registered it as "connection lost" — interpreted as: I didn't accept who she currently was.

This "cognitive gap" led to a fatal **"double bind"**: she had to painfully toggle between "being herself (being criticized by me)" and "performing the version I wanted (feeling exhausted)." For her, I was not a partner but an interviewer who was scoring her at every moment.

## III. The Tenderness Hidden by Logic: About the "Jelly Bean" Ratio

Yet beneath this precise logical machine, there was an extremely clumsy and deep devotion. Since I learned about the metaphor she loved — the tip of the ice cream cone — I quietly started saving the best part for her, and began complimenting her beauty. These behaviors were completely contrary to the efficiency logic of "optimization," yet they were one of the few emotional languages I had learned.

But the devastating truth is: **the weight of negative emotions is usually several times that of positive ones.**

I gave her ten strawberry-flavored jelly beans — representing companionship, play, and care — but inadvertently mixed in one "cilantro-flavored" one — my criticism of her physique and daily habits. For someone who craved a sense of security, the taste of that one overwhelmed all the good ones. In the logical world, that was just a minor bug. But in the realm of emotion, it was an execution of the soul.

## IV. Learning to Be "Clumsy": Emotional Acceptance Before Diagnosis

I always tried to complete the "logical diagnosis" before finishing the "emotional acceptance." When she expressed grievance, my first reaction was to think and analyze, believing that as long as I ensured the communication channel was open, I could resolve emotions through rational means.

But in truth, what she may have needed wasn't a verbal analysis — it was a hug, or just a simple "I know it's been hard on you."

My "clumsy love" doesn't mean I loved too little; it's the posture of someone still learning. I admit that some things don't need to be optimized, and some emotions don't need to be understood rationally. I used to think the endpoint of communication was reaching consensus. In reality, sometimes communication exists simply to confirm: even though we are so different, we are still willing to stay in this connection.

Tragically, the carefully constructed communication channel — the first time it was used was also the last.
