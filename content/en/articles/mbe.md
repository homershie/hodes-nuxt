  <h2>The Rise of Flat Design</h2>
  <p>
    In recent years, design has increasingly favored flat, minimal, and abstract aesthetics. This shift may stem from cultural, social, and other deeper factors—too many to summarize in a few sentences. Below are two simplified points based on my own reasoning and on design history and trends introduced by Professor Feng during university lectures. These are speculative and not backed by statistics, so take them as reference only:
  </p>

  <h3>1. Faster, and Even Faster</h3>
  <p>
    With Industry 4.0, demand for visuals in web, marketing, and UI has surged. Design and creativity must be more efficient and productive to meet these needs. As a result, time-consuming, elaborate, highly decorative post-modern styles have given way to simpler, quicker approaches.
    Note: Modernism also favoured simple, geometric shapes, but the visual elements discussed here keep Modernism’s simplicity without its dehumanizing edge, while retaining Post-modern playfulness. Think of it like having a serious grandfather and a playful father—you need to be sometimes serious, sometimes playful 😆
  </p>

  <h3>2. Leave Me Alone</h3>
  <p>
    Terms like “information overload” and “big data” appear constantly in the media. People today process far more information than in the agricultural era, and technology keeps widening that gap. After a day of information overload, what do you want to do when you get home? Collapse into bed and binge-watch until you fall asleep, then repeat the next day. Simple, clear visuals work best in this context—they quickly convey what an ad or message is about in the few seconds before you scroll past.
  </p>
  <p>
    Professor Feng called this flat, minimal, abstract approach “Light Modern” (輕現代)—a style that blends Modern and Post-modern aesthetics—and predicted it would become a dominant design direction.
  </p>

  <h2>What Is MBE?</h2>
  <p>
    MBE style belongs to this Light Modern category. The name comes from a designer on Dribbble known as MBE, whose simple, playful work became influential. MBE style typically has these characteristics:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>Flat design with no sense of depth</li>
    <li>Thick, rounded, black broken outlines</li>
    <li>Organic deformation of geometric shapes</li>
    <li>High value, high saturation color palettes</li>
    <li>Slightly offset fill (fill shifted a bit to one side)</li>
    <li>No gradients</li>
    <li>Chibi/cute cartoon aesthetics (think :D)</li>
    <li>Small geometric decorative elements: stars, crosses, circles, radial lines, etc.</li>
    <li>Fast vector-based workflow</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    The conventional understanding of MBE style includes the nine points above. However, recent MBE work on Dribbble shows experiments with gradient fills and removal of the signature black outlines—possibly an attempt to evolve the style. Worth keeping an eye on.
  </p>

  <h2>Summary</h2>
  <p>
    MBE style works well for web and UI visuals. Because it’s simple to produce, it’s also cost-effective for animation. One concern: the style has little cultural depth—it’s easy to assume any MBE piece was made by a foreign designer. It’s a product of tech culture, but that also makes it <strong>well-suited to tech and internet industries</strong>. To avoid viewers assuming you’re a foreign designer, consider putting more effort into culturally specific character design. I’d love to see someone depict Taiwanese pig blood cake (豬血糕) in MBE style 😋
  </p>
