Last December, I stood barefoot on a competition stage. Oiled skin, muscle definition scored down to the decimal, a year and a half of anti-entropy behavior compressed into a few short minutes. After stepping off stage, I had a photo shoot — the finished shots were among the rare moments I felt like "this body has actually been seen." The person in those photos stood firm, eyes steady.

Five months later, those same feet were in sneakers, standing inside a dimly lit bar in East District — and they didn't know where to step.

What's the difference?

Over the past few days, Claude and I tried to take this question apart. What follows is what we slowly peeled back.

## 1. A Social Community Without a Physical Anchor

Gym friends — let's call them that — occupy a slightly unusual category.

Gym friends are relationships built through **shared practice**. The bond forms because you suffer under the same weights together, watch each other change slowly in the mirror. Without the weight, without the mirror, the origin point of the relationship simply doesn't exist.

On Thursday night, seven of us headed to a bar in East District. We'd known each other about a year on average. At that timescale you might assume we're close — but which version of each other are we actually close to? We'd never tested what this connection looks like outside the gym. The indulgence of a bar sits in direct contrast with the kind of relationship gym friends have, one built on self-discipline. That contrast made me feel both unsettled and curious.

## 2. Stillness in the Noise and Clumsy Steps Forward

Going to an East District bar for the first time felt like walking into a club for the first time — I didn't know what I was supposed to be doing. Dim lights, loud music, the gaps between tables just wide enough that you couldn't quite hear what others were saying but could absolutely feel yourself being sized up. Four of us introverts found a spot and started talking among ourselves.

It wasn't until the full group arrived an hour later that the energy started expanding outward, and some people's eyes began scanning the room. The one who actually made a move that night was my closest friend in the group. He walked over to the table next to us and asked if they'd like to share a pizza — a small invitation, clumsy but sincere. I watched him do it, and something subtle shifted inside me. No judgment either way — just a kind of detached observation. Watching someone try to move forward, while I chose to stay still.

Then I watched him, someone with a lot going for him, get turned down. A sentence floated up in my mind: *guys in this scene really do feel like second-class citizens.*

That night I had two reactions running in parallel. One was the immediate feeling: I felt cheapened by this environment. The other was my brain instantly finding a foothold for that feeling: "This game is rigged. I don't want in." In the moment, I thought both sentences were true. But the more I thought about it on the way home, the more something felt off — like there was something I'd tucked away behind them. I'll leave that unease here and come back to it.

## 3. The Recovery Progress Bar

The friend who walked over and asked about the pizza — he's a front desk receptionist at the gym, someone I met when I signed my annual membership over a year ago.

He went through a short but intense relationship that ended a few months before mine did. The breakup was painful, but clean. About a week after his, I asked him out to a movie, wanting to understand what he was going through and check in on him. Later, when it was my turn, he came to find me and talk me through it. We traded feelings that only someone who'd been through something similar could decode. He's one of the few people who could genuinely hear what I was describing and feel it too.

He processed his relationship through songwriting; I processed mine through a handful of late-night articles. We each had our own chisel, carving away at our own piece of stone.

When he walked over and asked about the pizza last night, he had already moved further along than me. His pain had been cut off by a definitive block, so he could start walking again. Watching him do it, I could see clearly that I wasn't there yet — and that we were healing in different ways.

## 4. Social Availability ≠ Inner Readiness

I'd set 2 AM as my personal cutoff. Leaving and calling a car, my head was clear — not drunk, just vaguely adrift, unsure what I'd been doing.

The next day I tried to explain this state to Claude. In the first round, we proposed a framework that sounded clever: "the builder facing an improv prompt" — the idea being that someone like me, who's used to slow accumulation, hadn't practiced the improvised kind of interaction a bar requires. Claude ran with it smoothly, but I felt it couldn't hold up. I've played guitar, played basketball, my reaction time isn't that bad. It's not that I lack the capacity for improvisation.

Claude admitted on its own that "builder" wasn't a real explanation — it was just a psychologically convenient escape route. The actual reason was more specific and personal: **it's not that I "can't" make a move. It's that "now isn't the right time."**

Financially, I had just started a new job and was reorganizing things (vol.01 worked through this theme — this time it's the practical continuation). Psychologically it was even clearer — my last relationship had ended just a month prior, and I'd only just finished clarifying my new role in my own life. In this sequence, my "social availability" might still be there, but my "inner readiness" was simply not online.

Looking back at the two reactions from that night — feeling cheapened, feeling like the environment was unfair — they were actually the same thing, readable in two ways. One reading is true: that environment genuinely is structured against men, and what I saw was a real structural reality. The other is rationalization: I used the seemingly objective "the environment is unfair" to package the harder-to-admit "I wasn't ready" into a detached-sounding critique.

Both readings can coexist. Saying "the environment is unfair" out loud sounds sharper, cooler — but admitting "I wasn't ready" requires a certain degree of self-disclosure. More warmth to it. These are actually two separate things. You can joke around with friends and engage with people in that environment — but if your inner state isn't ready, you'll always be the outside observer, no matter how physically close you are.

## 5. An Unreadable Afterimage

My relationship before last ended with a direct block — painful, but clean. I thought I'd learned that kind of closure. Then this breakup happened: she said she couldn't continue, that she just wanted to be friends. My first instinct was to block her too, but I decided not to — half because I thought a slow fade might hurt her less, less likely to leave her with guilt; half because maybe I didn't want her to disappear from my world entirely either. But the cost of a slow fade is a different shape of pain.

I've genuinely come to think of her as someone I know online. I occasionally reply to the stories I want to reply to; she occasionally hearts mine, we exchange a few lines, and it usually ends with me left on read, the conversation cutting off at awkward places, the distance clearly felt. Lately I find myself circling between two contradictory thoughts. One: has she actually moved on? The other: she's a sentimental person, and deliberately not reaching out might be her way of protecting us — she does still reply, a line or two. Both versions hold. Neither holds. And there's no way to verify either.

All I can do is admit I'm stuck in this **unreadable** state — and it was precisely that being stuck that made me unable to make any forward movement last night.

At the end of vol.05, I wrote: "She'll carry a shadow of me into her future." I later realized that goes both ways, and the effect lasts much longer than I estimated. One of the reasons I became an outside observer last night might be living inside that shadow — not lingering feelings, just a shadow that hasn't dispersed yet. Before it fully clears, meeting someone new would only hurt everyone involved, and harm whatever comes next. So right now, I'd rather stay quietly in place for a while.

## Closing: Bare Feet, Sneakers, and the Aftershocks of a Disconnection

Back to those bare feet at the start. The feet on the competition stage last December knew where the light was, where the judges were, how to position the muscle. Five months later, the feet in sneakers inside an East District bar had no idea where to step. I'd walked from an environment where "results can be precisely scored" into one where I didn't know the rules — and had no interest in learning them right now.

But him walking over to ask about the pizza, and me quietly leaving at two in the morning — those are just two coordinates on the same map. He made his move, maybe just wanting to test his own weight in this city. I chose "not yet," simply because I'm still wrapping something up. We're both still processing, each paused at a different stage. Neither of us has actually finished.

After seeing it that way, I realized — that bar in East District, and the chat window on my phone, are essentially the same space. Her occasional hearts might just be a kind of tenderness and sentimentality — no different from a nod across a bar, both carrying no promise of "a future." For someone who habitually digs into everything, every attempt to "just go with it," every click and speculation, is donating your cognitive bandwidth — for free — to a black hole that will never have an answer.

The AI in the chat window told me: "What you should do right now is quietly put that hand — the one hovering toward the heart button and the comment box — back in your pocket." Its algorithm is airtight. It really is the optimal solution for protecting a system from overconsumption. But it has no body. It doesn't know what it feels like when a finger hovers above the screen, that tiny but heavy gravitational pull.

In the dim light of the East District bar, I held the physical boundary. Facing the chat window, I know what the most rational answer is. **As for whether these hands — tomorrow, the day after, or some future late night — can put themselves back in my pocket with the precision of a machine: that's the aftershock I have to wait out alone, after cutting the connection.**
