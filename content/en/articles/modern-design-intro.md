  <p>
    When first reading about Western cultural movements, many get confused by all the "moderns." This series helps clarify the impact of various modern movements on visual communication and introduces the emerging cultural sensibility—metamodernism—so you can not only understand design history but also apply it practically to your projects.
  </p>
  <p>
    This guide simplifies visual styles and sidesteps intricate historical contexts and branches by grouping them into four major movements: <strong>Traditional Era, Modern, Postmodern, and Metamodern</strong>. It focuses on brief descriptions of each movement's character and visual language. Follow-up articles will cover the formation, evolution, and historical and cultural background of each movement. Stay tuned for updates if you're interested!

  <h2>The Classic Old Soul — Traditional Era (Early 19th Century – Mid-20th Century)</h2>
  <p>
    Before introducing modernism, we must first understand the classical and traditional. The Traditional Era began with the Second Industrial Revolution, when mechanical printing replaced manual printing and spurred the development of printed publications. By the early 20th century, modernism had emerged in the visual field, yet traditional styles continued to evolve until around mid-century. You can still observe the influence of the Traditional Era in old storefronts in Taiwan today.
  </p>

    <img

  <p>
    In this era, design was not yet a profession. Visual publications were typically produced by formally trained artists, so they remained tied to representational painting and various ornate visual elements, some clearly influenced by Rococo. Well-known styles include Victorian Style, Arts & Crafts, and Art Nouveau. The Traditional Era as defined here differs somewhat from standard design history texts; it reflects a personal view of what belongs in this category. For example, propaganda posters from the world wars (Heroic Realism), American Kitsch Style, Old Shanghai-style posters, and early Taiwanese packaging all fall under this umbrella. These designs were shaped by different national traditions and cultures, so they varied around the world.
  </p>

  <h3>Visual Characteristics of the Traditional Period:</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Representational art and painting, with organic curves or botanical motifs</li>
    <li>Highly decorative visual elements or typography</li>
    <li>Predominantly mid-to-high brightness, low saturation, and analogous color schemes</li>
    <li>Orderly or symmetrical composition</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Straight-A Student — Modernism (Early 20th Century – Mid-Century)</h2>

  <p>
    Modernism did not emerge out of nowhere. From its philosophical foundations to its impact on the visual field, there was an experimental period in the arts before modernism took shape. That period—and the decades that followed—continues to influence visual design today. Styles such as British Art Nouveau, Art Deco, Cubism, Dada, Futurism, and Surrealism laid the groundwork for modernism.

  <p>
    Drawing on these artistic influences, modernist design flourished around the world in the early 20th century—a kind of synchronic phenomenon. This era was built on science, rationality, and maximum utility. It boldly embraced new materials and technologies and drew a clear line with tradition, applying scientific methods to design and breaking down form, color, material, and space. As a result, geometric shapes and primary colors became central design elements, as seen in Russian Constructivism, De Stijl, Bauhaus, and Swiss/International Style. It also gave rise to Functionalism and Minimalism.
  </p>
  <h3>Visual Characteristics of the Modern Period:</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Geometric shapes and line-based elements</li>
    <li>Little or no decorative elements</li>
    <li>Predominantly high brightness and high saturation; emphasis on primary and secondary colors</li>
    <li>Rigorous, orderly composition</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The Rebellious Teen — Postmodernism (Mid-20th Century – Present)</h2>
  <p>
    Postmodernism can be described as the visually rebellious teenager. It clearly differs from modernism in appearance. Most would agree that postmodernism emerged from post-WWII skepticism toward rationality and logic, and from postwar critiques of capitalism and industrial society. This era produced the hippie culture that advocated love and peace, and rock culture that questioned or even rejected reason.

  <p>
    This era embraced humanity and nature and rejected rational geometric elements entirely. Many vibrant visual works emerged during this period: Pop Art and Op Art—postmodern pioneers that widely used collage and celebrated popular culture; Deconstruction—an alchemy of deconstructing and reconstructing; Fractal Art—computer art based on chaos theory; trippy Psychedelic Art; Grunge Style with its post-apocalyptic feel, seen in games like Fallout and films like Mad Max, which directly mimicked rough, gritty textures; and various graffiti art, music, and anti-war posters. Postmodern influence on visual culture persists today. Beyond these classic styles, recent trends such as Vaporwave/Synthwave and Glitch Art bear a postmodern visual imprint. There is also work that embraces "ugly" as a design approach—some call it New Ugly—which will be covered in a future article in this series. Consider this a cliffhanger for now.
  </p>
  <figure class="image">
    <img

  <p>
    In commercial applications—logos, for example—design moved away from the geometric emphasis of the modern period toward fluid or organic forms and realistic textures. The automotive industry, for instance, used simulated embossing and metallic finishes on logos. Effects commonly seen in Photoshop—shadows, bevels, gradients, glows—were frequently used in commercial work during this period. The contrast is evident when comparing logos of older companies.
  </p>
  <h3>Visual Characteristics of the Postmodern Period:</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Organic shapes or photomontage</li>
    <li>Heavy use of decorative elements</li>
    <li>Bold color for emotional impact; strong black-white or color contrast</li>
    <li>Random, sometimes chaotic composition</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>The Pragmatic Chameleon — Metamodernism (Early 21st Century – Present)</h2>
  <p>
    Neomodernism, Late Modernism / Liquid Modernism, Remodernism, Post-postmodernism, Hypermodernism, Altermodernism, Digimodernism, Light Modern—and more untranslated terms like Cosmodernism, Performatism, Automodernism, Transmodernism, Renewalism, and so on. If your English is strong and you enjoy academic papers, you can search these keywords. All of these fall under Metamodernism. Personal recommendation: Liquid Modernity is worth exploring.
  </p>
  <p>
    To clarify: metamodernism is a philosophical and academic field that has been taking shape in the 21st century. It is often seen as the zeitgeist that follows postmodernism. Similar frameworks existed before metamodernism, but current online discourse mainly uses Hypermodernism and Metamodernism. I use "Metamodernism" here because I don't want to encounter yet another term like Ultramodernism later—it would be like designers endlessly renaming "final" files, and just as hard to translate.
  </p>
  <p>
    Metamodernism is often defined as a sensibility or visual expression that sits between modernism and postmodernism. I picture modernism as the rigid, logic-driven scientist grandfather; postmodernism as the emotionally rich artist father who clashes with grandpa; and metamodernism as the son who refuses to be as extreme as either, weathered by life and diplomatic, still figuring himself out.
  </p>

  <p>
    I currently group several styles under metamodernism: Late Modern Style and Memphis Design, which evolved from modern design; digitization-era styles such as Pixel Art and Isometric design; context-driven styles like Flat Design, including its offshoots MBE style and Flat 3D; and newer media approaches such as Laser/Fluid gradient and Motion Graphics. Flat illustration with texture, increasingly seen in design today, also fits the metamodern umbrella. Digitalization has given design new workflows through personal computers and enabled virtualization at scale. As technology advances, real-world elements may extend into VR and various devices, marking a new era.
  </p>
  <h3>Visual Characteristics of the Metamodern Period:</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Mix of geometric and organic shapes; avoiding overly realistic forms</li>
    <li>Moderate use of decorative elements</li>
    <li>Predominantly high brightness and mid-to-high saturation</li>
    <li>Random yet harmonious composition</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Conclusion</h2>
  <p>
    For tech company projects, I lean toward a modernist approach; for museum exhibitions, I draw from the Traditional Era; in Taiwan, you often see postmodern influence in music merchandise. These movements continue to shape the present in different forms. So, to answer the title—"Contemporary, Modern, Postmodern, Hypermodern, Metamodern: Which Is Now?"—I'd say they are all present and ongoing. Combined with new technology, this eclectic mix of visual languages defines today and is a major source of inspiration for me. Thinking fluidly across perspectives and oscillating between different sensibilities helps respond to rapid change.
  </p>
  <p>
    Though I've labeled each movement with its active period and preferred design language for clarity, all these styles still influence the present. Because each creator's approach differs, design languages can only be roughly classified. I hope this article gives you a basic understanding of mainstream design movements.
