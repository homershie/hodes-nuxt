## I. The Paradox of Freedom

When I was younger, I hated entering any framework or institution. So I chose freelancing—chose not to join a company, chose to "control my own work rhythm." It felt like resistance, like rebellion. But freedom has a strange quality: **freedom carries a hidden exchange; it bites back when you least expect it.**

When you rest too long, economics and survival start limiting your choices. Without visibility, your client pool shrinks. "Freelance by choice" becomes "freelance by necessity." You thought you escaped the frame—instead you fell into a more invisible one: **the frame of economics.**

The cruel irony: now I'm "actively" entering the very system I once fled. That choice wounds my pride. It feels like betraying my younger self, like falling short of my own expectations.

Claude offered a view: **True freedom was never "no constraints"—it's "the power to choose which constraints."** The real question: which form of unfreedom serves you better right now? Admitting this is hard, because it means admitting **"pure freedom" was always an illusion.**

## II. The Inevitability of the Capital Game

Some say: "I don't want to play by capital's rules. I'll do research, art, literature." But is that path really outside capital?

Scientists write proposals to please reviewers and secure funding. Artists cater to gallery taste, waiting for collectors. **Every "pure" path gets absorbed by capital in some form.** The difference isn't "whether you rely on capital" but "what your goals require."

Commercial logic handles "known needs"—companies pay because they need solutions now. Academia and art handle "exploring the unknown"—they allow short-term experiments with no immediate return. But that gap is narrowing: academia tracks citations, art is commercializing, literature even more so.

**So the question was never "which path is outside the system"—it's "which costs and tradeoffs can you accept."** Every path is value exchange. Scientists trade research for funding, artists trade work for recognition, you trade time for salary. Acknowledging this isn't surrender—it's seeing the rules clearly so you can play in your favor.

## III. Exploring the Unknown vs. Cog Anxiety

"After joining a company, my code belongs to the company, the product belongs to the company, the achievement belongs to the team. I'm just a replaceable cog." That anxiety comes from a deeper need—**to be remembered by the world.**

Illustrators have style; animators get credits. But frontend engineers? No one knows who wrote that Vue component. Your contribution becomes invisible infrastructure.

I asked Claude: "Can't I get the same growth from side projects? Why must I join a company?" Claude replied: **What I want isn't just technical growth—I want to confirm that "my existence has meaning."**

But there's a trap: I've tied "meaning" to "personal attribution." If I build something useful and no one knows I made it, that thing still has meaning. **Meaning doesn't come from ownership; it comes from impact.** The real blockage is mistaking meaningful work for fame.

## IV. What Company Experience Actually Offers

Technically, the gap between company work and side projects isn't as large as I imagined. I know how to learn React, how to write Three.js. But companies offer three things side projects can't:

**First, scale experience.** My side project might have 100 users; a company product might have 100,000 or a million. Performance bottlenecks, error handling, edge cases—the complexity scales exponentially. Only under real traffic do you learn how code actually breaks.

**Second, collaboration friction.** Your own project, your own rules. In a company you negotiate with PM, design, backend, QA, even legal and security. That's the skill of "delivering under constraints and compromise."

**Third, social proof.** "Frontend engineer at X" vs. "10 side projects"—employers weigh them differently. "Someone pays steady salary" is credible proof.

**A company is a high-leverage trade—time and autonomy for an accelerator.** You could accumulate equivalent experience through side projects, but you'd need to find your own traffic, handle scale yourself, prove yourself to the world. That's hard, and slow.

## V. Cogs Come in All Shapes—The Engineer's "Cool"

I thought frontend dev had no style, no personal signature like illustration. But that's because I'm still in the "using tools" phase, not "creating tools."

Evan You is a frontend engineer; he created Vue. Dan Abramov created Redux. Sarah Drasner defined animation CSS aesthetics. **They all have strong personal style—just expressed differently.**

An engineer's individuality isn't "uniqueness"—it's "reliability + aesthetics." The person who makes complex animations smooth and beautiful, the one who catches key issues in code review—that's style too, just less visual. **Joining a company isn't being a cog—it's proving your methodology works in a high-stakes environment.**

In side projects you can build anything cool; no one asks "will this crash at 100K users?" or "is the maintenance cost too high?" The company is a brutal proving ground—**if you can write elegant, efficient, maintainable code under harsh conditions, that's a kind of cool.** You just need to reframe what "cool" means.

## Conclusion

To be human is to be thrown into constraints: the body ages, we need money, recognition, meaning. I didn't choose to be born; I can't pick my starting conditions like in a game. Existence itself is absurd.

Camus said the only serious philosophical question is suicide—after realizing life's absurdity, do you keep playing? **My resentment is this: "Why must I play a game I never agreed to?"** That resentment won't disappear, and it shouldn't. It's proof of awareness.

But after admitting the absurdity, you still have to decide how to play. **The question was never "which path is outside capital"—it's "which path lets you play this forced game with enough dignity and style."** Every path is exchange; all freedom is limited; all meaning is self-assigned. Right now, **I believe meaning comes from impact—the ripples I create in the world.** May you find yours too.
