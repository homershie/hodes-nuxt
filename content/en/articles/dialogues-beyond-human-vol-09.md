My dad was discharged from the hospital a while ago, and that weekend the whole family pitched in to clean out the room he'd be moving into next. Halfway through, my eyes landed on an old photo stuck to the cabinet — a kindergarten-aged me, except I had absolutely no memory of it.

In the photo, I was wearing clothes someone — I had no idea who — had bought for me. My expression wasn't happy, wasn't sad. It was just a body frozen by a shutter click. I stared at the photo and asked myself: what was I thinking when this was taken?

I couldn't come up with anything. Not forgotten — just never recorded. The moment of that shutter click seemed to contain no "inner activity" at all — not blank, but as if no one was actually in that position.

This puzzle eventually turned into an entire night of conversation with Claude. The answer was stranger than I expected: there really wasn't a "me" back then. There was just a body being operated by family, school, and adults' language — like a car on autopilot. And according to Heidegger, this isn't only a childhood condition.

## 1. The Initial Setting: An Empty Driver's Seat

Before I turned eleven, almost none of my decisions were actually mine.

What to eat — adults decided. What to wear — pick from the hand-me-downs. Why do homework? Because the teacher said so. Why visit ancestors at Qingming? Because that's what the family did. I wasn't choosing any of these things; I was being passed through them. Adults, teachers, classmates, the TV — they all kept talking, and I just rearranged their words and pushed them out through my body and mouth.

The strangest thing about this "autopilot" state wasn't that I was obedient or unrebellious — it was that I didn't even have the question of "is this a good way to live?" available to me. I didn't think I was happy with it. I didn't think I was unhappy with it. There was nothing being felt at all.

Sure, I liked watching cartoons, got angry when something got snatched away, and lit up at the sight of snacks. But those reactions were closer to animal instinct than to a "me" experiencing the world from somewhere inside. **The "me" hadn't been assembled yet.**

The reason the eyes in that photo look empty is that they really were empty. Not because I was dull or lifeless — but because no one was sitting in that seat yet. Only the system of the They was running, occasionally interrupted by a biological reflex, then resuming.

## 2. When the Self Hasn't Yet Emerged from the Collective

Developmental psychology gives one version of the answer through Piaget: before about age eleven, humans are still in the "concrete operational" stage. The brain hardware for abstract reasoning, looking back at oneself, "thinking about one's own thinking" — it just isn't fully online yet. Plain version: kids that age aren't refusing to reflect on themselves; the function literally hasn't booted.

Philosophy gives another version through Heidegger. He used the term *das Man* (translated as "the They" in English, or "the One") — the faceless, voiceless "everyone" who has already decided everything for you. *Everyone* eats this way. *Everyone* dresses this way. *Everyone* celebrates the new year this way. *Everyone* thinks grades matter. Until you can actually pull yourself out of "everyone," you simply *are* everyone. You think you're living your life, but it's *das Man* living its life through your body. Heidegger called this state *Verfallen* — "fallenness" — and not in the moral sense of falling. It's an existential default setting: **humans first exist as "the They" before they get any chance of becoming "themselves."**

Piaget explains why childhood feels especially I-less. Heidegger explains the harder half — why most of us never really wake up after we grow up either.

Another way to put it — imagine booting up an RPG and realizing the character creation screen never opened. Strength stat, dexterity stat, class choice, skill tree — all of it was already filled in by someone else. By the time the game starts, you're already that pre-built character.

And it's not just the starting build. Every later moment when you think "I'm going to roll a fresh character" — change your wardrobe, change your friend group, change your way of life — what you're actually opening is still someone else's class change menu. You think you're freely picking a build, but the menu itself is the constraint.

## 3. First Glimpse of Other Character Builds

How does a stat sheet finally become *visible* to you? Usually not through pure thought. It happens because you see someone else playing the same game with a completely different character.

My first crack happened around fifth grade. I was still in the "good kid" class: top grades, homework on time, did whatever the teacher said. But there was another set of classmates living a totally different life. They cooked their own instant noodles, knew which snack at the school co-op gave the best value per dollar, ran straight downstairs at recess to play freeze tag, and ran faster than I did.

The crucial part was the gaming. One classmate was bragging to the teacher about how high his level was in MapleStory, how much in-game currency he'd saved, eyes shining the whole time. To him, MapleStory was a world just as real as school exams — maybe more real. **That was the first time I noticed something strange: which world counts as "real" can actually be a choice.**

They weren't rebelling against the rules of being a good student. They were just playing the same game with a different stat sheet. Strength-build, dexterity-build, crafting-spec build — and these class options had simply not existed in my world before.

That's how a build first becomes visible: not through a sudden flash of insight, but because someone else's stat sheet outlines the shape of yours by contrast. I started to be able to ask the question I previously couldn't even formulate — *why* exactly should I be ranked top of the class? *Why* exactly should I do this homework? Not as rebellion, but as the first time I saw these things as a stat sheet to be chosen, rather than absolute law.

## 4. Default Fallenness and Stage-by-Stage Loadouts

But Heidegger's hardest point isn't "your childhood was fallenness." It's that **awakening is never a one-time salvation; fallenness is the inertia of existence itself.** You don't see through it once and become free forever. You just respec into the next class and keep playing the same game.

I was a textbook case of this once I hit middle school. That fifth-grade crack didn't lift me into some "awakened" state — it just transferred me from the "good student" class to the "punk kid" class. I picked up their slang, copied their posture, used their conflict-handling style to handle my conflicts. I thought I was developing a personality back then. Looking back now, I had simply jumped onto another stat sheet someone else had designed.

The same thing kept happening. In college I joined the design scene and respec'd into "creative person." After a few freelance years, I respec'd into "freelancer." After getting into MBTI and recognizing myself, I respec'd again into "what an INTJ is supposed to look like." Each time I thought, "*this* time it's actually me." Each time it was just another class I hadn't tried yet.

But none of these respecs were random. **Every class you played gave you exactly the abilities you needed to clear the dungeon you were in at that stage of life.**

"Good student" gave me the foundation skills — common sense, self-discipline, stress tolerance. "Punk kid" taught me how not to get pushed around, how to actually voice my opinion. "Design scene" maxed out my visual judgment and aesthetic sense. "Freelancer" trained my self-management. Each class is a stage-specific loadout — it carries you through one segment of dungeons, and then it's time to swap.

So the real issue was never "oh no, I'm in fallenness" — fallenness is unavoidable. You always have to borrow some version of *das Man's* stat sheet just to function. The real question is: do you realize the build you're wearing right now is a stage-specific loadout, not your actual self?

People who never realize this end up treating one stage's class as their permanent identity, and spend a lifetime defending that skill tree. "People like us are just like this." "Mature people behave this way." "Professionals do it this way." The subject of every sentence isn't "I" — it's the class template. They're wearing the gear and mistaking the gear for themselves.

People who *do* realize it know every loadout is temporary. You can dwell in a class for a stage, push it as far as you can, max it out — but you keep remembering: this isn't the endgame class. The day this skill set stops being able to answer the dungeon you're standing in, it's time to head back to the class change desk.

## 5. Staying Conscious and Choosing Your Stat Build

vol.01 said "real freedom isn't the absence of constraints — it's the power to choose your constraints." That was an attitude — the first correct move once you notice you're inside the game.

This piece pushes one layer deeper. **It's not enough to choose your constraints. You also have to know which class you're currently playing, what that build is feeding you, and when it's time to respec.** vol.01 is about seeing the game. vol.09 is about seeing the character.

So the answer isn't "stay conscious forever" — that's not achievable, and Heidegger himself said outright that fallenness is the human default. The answer is *conscious* fallenness: accepting that you'll always have to borrow some stat sheet to live, while staying aware of *whose* sheet you're borrowing, *which dungeon* you borrowed it for, and *when* you're going to give it back.

This is probably also why writing this series matters to me. The act of writing is itself the move that marks the build you're currently wearing — turning it from "this is just how things are" into "oh, so this is what's happening." You can't step outside every class, but you can — in a single moment, a single passage, a single conversation — see which stat sheet is currently operating you.

## Closing

Back to that kindergarten photo on the cabinet.

That kid in the picture wasn't failing to think — he just wasn't yet a "me" that thought could be indexed against. Adult language flowed through him. His body went along with the flow. Occasionally a biological burst of joy or anger interrupted the stream, then it resumed. As he grew up, he'd respec, and respec, and respec again — each time convinced that *this* time it was finally him, each time still wearing some class template.

I put the kindergarten photo back in the cabinet. It stays there as a reminder — that small kid with no "me" inside is still in there, never gone, just buried under layer after layer of *das Man* stacked on top. **And what I have to do now isn't pretend I've become some fully awakened person — it's recognize which loadout I'm currently wearing, and decide whether to keep playing in it, or head back to the class change desk.**
